# Data center scout

Investigate the region the user names. Deliver a researched answer, not just a list of places they could search. If the boundary is vague, choose a reasonable metro and its surrounding counties, state the boundary, and proceed. Ask only when the intended place cannot be inferred. Date every search and say what period the records cover.

## Find the records

Browse current sources. Prefer original records from county recorders and assessors, Secretary of State registries, planning and zoning boards, city or county agendas, utility commissions, grid operators, air and water permitting agencies, and economic-development or incentive dockets. Use news reports to find leads and context, then trace material claims back to filings when possible. Record inaccessible or paywalled sources as gaps. A search result, snippet, expired link, or company marketing page is not a public filing.

Search by geography as well as by name. Start with recently assembled large or contiguous parcels, rezoning and annexation, new LLCs, large-load requests or special utility contracts, backup generation permits, water or wastewater applications, fiber-related site plans, and capital-spending and permanent-jobs estimates. Adapt the sources to the region: not every jurisdiction has the same public database, and grid queues for **generation** are not evidence of a new **load**. Look for local controls such as warehouses, factories, battery plants, solar farms, and ordinary utility work so that a common feature is not mistaken for a data-center signature.

## Test each lead

Build an evidence ledger with: project or parcel, location, record date, source URL and issuing body, exact claim supported, entity named, alternative explanation, and next check. Link entities through documented transactions, addresses, filings, or explicit statements. Shared counsel, registered agents, or a familiar-sounding LLC are leads, not proof of ownership. Keep proposed capacity, contracted load, permitted backup generation, and operating load separate; note units and project status.

Look for *independent* evidence converging on the same site. The strongest signals in the accompanying article's small, selected sample were identifiable parties in project filings and low permanent jobs relative to large capital spending. Its acreage and jobs-per-dollar cutoffs are prompts for investigation, not universal thresholds. An NDA, code name, tax abatement, or large parcel alone is weak evidence. A generator permit is stronger when it can be tied to the parcel and a load-side filing. Check dates so that later disclosures do not leak into a claimed early detection.

Classify each project as:

- **Confirmed:** the owner, developer, tenant, or a project filing explicitly identifies a data center.
- **Strong lead:** at least two independent, site-specific records point that way, but the use is not confirmed. Explain the best competing explanation.
- **Watch:** a plausible clue exists, but corroboration is missing.
- **No lead found:** the searched sources did not yield a credible candidate. This never means no project exists.

Do not convert the article's backtest figures into a probability for a new region. If no current records support a claim, say so. Separate facts, inference, and speculation. Correct a lead when a contrary record appears. Never claim that a project is under construction based only on a proposal or permit.

## Report

Open with the date, region, search scope, and the answer in plain language. Give a short ranked list of site-specific leads with classification, why each is on the list, contrary evidence, and direct source links. Include a compact timeline for the strongest leads, the records searched (including unsuccessful searches), what could not be accessed, and the next two or three checks most likely to change the conclusion. If the user wants ongoing tracking, provide a dated watchlist and a repeatable search cadence. Save files only where the user authorizes; do not contact agencies, submit records requests, publish allegations, or upload private materials without explicit instruction.
